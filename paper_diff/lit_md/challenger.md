# HELIX: A Lifetime, Account-Level Loss Engine for Credit Card Portfolios

### Challenger model white paper — submitted for independent validation

Prepared by the Consumer Credit Modelling Group, Aldergate Financial Group
Lead author: **S. Venkataraman** · Contributors: **M. Okonjo**, **L. Přibyl**, **D. Ferraro**
Document ID ALD-CCM-WP-118 · Release candidate 3 · Classification: Internal

> **Purpose of this document.** HELIX is proposed as a challenger to the incumbent segment-level twelve-month loss rate model. This paper states the framework, reports empirical results on a nine-year account-month panel, and sets out the controls that would be required were HELIX to be promoted to production. It is written for a validation audience and assumes familiarity with the PD/EAD/LGD decomposition.

---

## Executive Summary

HELIX estimates credit card credit losses account by account and month by month over the full behavioural life of each account, rather than producing a single twelve-month loss rate for a risk segment. Loss is assembled as a discounted sum over monthly survival-weighted default events:

$$\text{Loss}_i = \sum_{m=1}^{M_i} S_i(m-1) \, h_i(m) \, \widehat{EAD}_i(m) \, \widehat{LGD}_i(m) \, (1+r)^{-m/12}.$$

The three factors are the same as in any expected-loss framework — a default probability, an exposure, and a loss severity — but each is a *function of account age and of a macroeconomic path* rather than a segment constant.

Headline findings on the 2015–2023 panel:

- Monthly hazard estimated by gradient-boosted trees attains an out-of-time AUC of **0.847** on the next-12-month default indicator, against **0.803** for a reproduction of the incumbent segment logit on the same sample.
- Lifetime loss rate for the 2023Q4 book is **8.94%** of outstanding balance at a 24-month behavioural life assumption, decomposing into 4.11% in the first twelve months and 4.83% thereafter.
- Exposure is simulated rather than scaled: the fitted balance path reproduces the observed pre-default run-up with a mean absolute error of 3.2% of limit, against 7.9% for a fixed credit conversion factor.
- Severity is estimated as a *discounted* recovery curve. Discounting at the effective interest rate raises portfolio LGD from 0.851 (nominal) to **0.879**.
- Forecast volatility is materially higher: the month-over-month standard deviation of the portfolio lifetime loss rate is 31 basis points, against 9 basis points for the incumbent.

The last bullet is the crux of the promotion decision and is treated at length in Part III.

## Part I — Why a Lifetime Account-Level Framework

### The horizon problem

A twelve-month loss rate answers a question that neither the accounting standard nor the pricing desk actually asks. Accounting requires expected credit losses over the expected life of the exposure; pricing requires the net present value of a relationship. The incumbent framework answers both by multiplying a twelve-month number by a judgmental lifetime factor, currently 2.18. That factor is a single scalar applied across a portfolio whose behavioural lives differ by a factor of four between the revolver and transactor populations.

Directly modelling the monthly timing of default removes the scalar. It also produces, as a by-product, the timing profile needed for interest-income projection and for the staging decisions that the incumbent cannot support.

### The aggregation problem

Segment-level estimation is unbiased for the segment mean and biased for anything else. Two consequences matter in practice:

1. **Within-segment heterogeneity is lost.** In the incumbent's 700–739 score band at 25–60% utilization, the account-level fitted twelve-month PD under HELIX ranges from 0.4% to 11.6% with an interquartile range of 1.9 percentage points. The incumbent assigns 2.84% to every account in the band.
2. **Population shift is invisible.** When the composition of a segment drifts, a segment-constant PD is stale by construction. The incumbent's own documentation reports a population stability index of 0.14 on the score distribution, above its warning threshold, with no correction mechanism.

### The factor-correlation problem

The product $PD \times EAD \times LGD$ of separately estimated factor means is not the mean of the product. Under stress all three factors deteriorate together, and the product form understates loss. The incumbent handles this with a judgmental additive overlay applied when projected unemployment change exceeds 200 basis points. HELIX instead conditions all three factors on the same macroeconomic path, so that the covariance is generated by the model rather than supplied by committee.

### Box 1 — What HELIX does not change

> HELIX retains the definition of default (180 days past due, bankruptcy, or long-term hardship with rate concession), the exclusion of private-label and small-business portfolios, and the 36-month recovery observation window. These are held fixed deliberately so that the comparison against the incumbent is attributable to specification rather than to scope.

## Part II — The Framework

### Notation and assembly

For account $i$ at months-on-book $m$ under macro path $\mathcal{M}$:

| Object | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $h_i(m)$ | Discrete-time default hazard: P(default in month $m$ \| alive at $m-1$) |
| $a_i(m)$ | Attrition hazard: P(voluntary closure in month $m$ \| alive and not defaulted) |
| $S_i(m)$ | Survival to end of month $m$, $\;S_i(m) = \prod_{k \le m}(1 - h_i(k))(1 - a_i(k))$ |
| $\widehat{EAD}_i(m)$ | Simulated balance conditional on default in month $m$ |
| $\widehat{LGD}_i(m)$ | Discounted loss severity for a default occurring in month $m$ |
| $M_i$ | Behavioural life cap (Section "Behavioural life") |
| $r$ | Discount rate, set to the account's effective interest rate |

Portfolio lifetime loss rate is $\sum_i \text{Loss}_i / \sum_i B_i$.

### Default hazard

The hazard is estimated on an account-month panel of 41.8 million rows (2015-01 through 2021-12 for development, 2022-01 through 2023-12 held out of time). The estimator is a gradient-boosted tree ensemble on the binary next-month default indicator, with months-on-book entered as a feature so that the baseline hazard shape is learned rather than imposed.

Feature families, with counts after selection:

| Family | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Delinquency dynamics | 11 | current bucket, worst bucket in 6m, cure count in 12m |
| Payment behaviour | 9 | payment/minimum ratio, 3m and 12m means, trend slope |
| Balance and utilization | 8 | utilization level, 6m change, cash-advance share |
| Bureau attributes | 7 | refreshed score, external trade delinquency, inquiry velocity |
| Account structure | 5 | months on book, limit, product, rate, promotional-balance flag |
| Macro (path-conditioned) | 4 | unemployment level and 12m change, debt service ratio, retail sales growth |

Hyperparameters were tuned by five-fold grouped cross-validation with accounts, not rows, as the grouping unit — a detail that matters, since row-wise folds leak an account's own future and inflated AUC by 0.03 in an early iteration of this work.

**Macro conditioning.** Macro features enter as contemporaneous values on the projected path. For forecasting, a macro path is supplied exogenously (baseline, adverse, severely adverse) and the hazard is re-evaluated month by month along that path. The model is therefore point-in-time by construction; no through-the-cycle adjustment is applied.

**Interpretability control.** Because a boosted ensemble is not inspectable coefficient by coefficient, monotonic constraints are imposed on eight features where credit intuition gives an unambiguous direction (utilization ↑, delinquency bucket ↑, payment ratio ↓, refreshed score ↓, unemployment ↑, debt service ratio ↑, cash-advance share ↑, inquiry velocity ↑). Constraining these costs 0.004 AUC and is judged worth the cost.

### Exposure simulation

HELIX does not use a credit conversion factor. Instead the balance path is simulated forward from a two-part model:

$$\Delta B_i(m) = \underbrace{\pi_i(m) \cdot (L_i - B_i(m-1))}_{\text{purchase/drawdown}} - \underbrace{\rho_i(m) \cdot B_i(m-1)}_{\text{payment}},$$

where the drawdown intensity $\pi$ and the payment rate $\rho$ are each fitted by quantile regression forests on the same feature set as the hazard, then evaluated at the conditional median. Balances are floored at zero and capped at $1.05 L_i$ to allow for over-limit fees.

Critically, $\pi$ and $\rho$ are fitted on the *pre-default* trajectory of accounts that subsequently defaulted as well as on survivors, with the default indicator excluded from the feature set to avoid look-ahead. The resulting paths reproduce the empirical run-up: simulated utilization rises from a median 0.63 twelve months before default to 0.97 in the default month, against observed 0.61 and 0.96.

**Table — exposure accuracy, out-of-time defaults**

| Method | MAE (% of limit) | Bias (% of limit) | Correlation with realized EAD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed CCF by utilization band | 7.9 | −1.4 | 0.781 |
| Fixed CCF by full segment cross | 7.1 | −1.1 | 0.804 |
| HELIX simulated path | 3.2 | +0.3 | 0.918 |

The improvement is concentrated in the 25–60% utilization population, where a band-constant CCF cannot distinguish a transactor drifting into revolving behaviour from a stable low-utilization revolver.

### Loss severity

Severity is built from a *recovery timing curve* rather than an ultimate recovery ratio. For each charged-off account, monthly collections are observed for up to 36 months. Two components are estimated:

- **Total nominal recovery**, by beta regression on resolution channel, exposure, bankruptcy flag and refreshed bureau score at charge-off.
- **Timing profile**, by a Dirichlet regression allocating total recovery across 36 monthly buckets, with the same covariates.

Discounted severity is then

$$LGD_i(m) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{36} w_{i,k} \, \hat{R}_i \, (1+r_i)^{-k/12},$$

with $w_{i,k}$ the fitted timing weights. Discounting at the account's effective interest rate rather than a portfolio rate is a deliberate choice: severity is used in pricing as well as in allowance, and a portfolio rate would cross-subsidize low-rate products.

**Table — severity by resolution channel, out-of-time charge-offs**

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Internal collections | 34% | 21.1% | 9.4 | 0.812 |
| Agency placement | 41% | 13.8% | 17.2 | 0.881 |

| Bankruptcy | 6% | 4.9% | 22.6 | 0.960 |

The 2.8 percentage point gap between nominal and discounted portfolio LGD is not a modelling refinement; it is a direct correction of an understatement in the incumbent, worth approximately 12 basis points of twelve-month loss rate and 26 basis points of lifetime loss rate.

### Behavioural life

A revolving account has no maturity, so the lifetime sum requires a horizon. HELIX sets $M_i$ endogenously as the month at which cumulative survival $S_i(m)$ falls below 0.05, capped at 120 months. Attrition hazard $a_i(m)$ is estimated by an analogous boosted model on the voluntary-closure indicator. The resulting balance-weighted mean behavioural life is 27.4 months, against the incumbent's implied 26.2 months (from its 2.18 lifetime factor) — reassuringly close in aggregate, but with far wider dispersion: the 10th percentile of account-level behavioural life is 8 months and the 90th is 61 months.

## Part III — Governance, Weaknesses and the Promotion Question

### Where HELIX is worse than the incumbent

We state these plainly, because the validation decision should not turn on discovering them later.

1. **Forecast volatility.** Month-over-month standard deviation of the portfolio lifetime loss rate is 31 bps, against 9 bps for the incumbent's twelve-month rate scaled by its lifetime factor. Roughly two-thirds of the additional variance traces to re-simulation of the balance paths, not to the hazard.
2. **Attribution difficulty.** A change in the reported loss rate cannot be decomposed into clean segment contributions. We provide Shapley-based attribution at feature-family level, but it is an approximation, and it is expensive: full attribution for the 7.4 million account book takes 4.2 hours on the current grid.
3. **Behavioural-life sensitivity.** Moving the survival cutoff from 0.05 to 0.02 raises the portfolio lifetime loss rate from 8.94% to 9.61%. There is no empirical basis in the data for preferring either threshold; the choice is a judgment, exactly like the incumbent's 2.18 factor, merely relocated.
4. **Macro path dependence.** Being point-in-time by construction, HELIX inherits all of the volatility of the supplied macro path. The incumbent's segment fixed effects damp this.
5. **Operational footprint.** Monthly production run is 6.1 hours versus 22 minutes. Three new upstream data feeds are required (monthly collections detail, bureau refresh at account level, promotional-balance tape).

### Where the incumbent is not salvageable

Against the above, three incumbent weaknesses cannot be fixed within its framework: the lifetime scalar, the absence of any within-segment differentiation, and the judgmental factor-correlation overlay. Each of these is a specification limit, not a calibration gap.

### Recommendation

We recommend HELIX be run in parallel for four consecutive quarters with monthly reporting of (a) portfolio lifetime and twelve-month loss rates against the incumbent, (b) the volatility decomposition of Part III item 1, and (c) segment-level reconciliation to the incumbent using the reporting view of Part II. We do not recommend promotion in this cycle. The volatility finding is real and unresolved, and we would rather resolve it before production than defend it afterwards.

### Required controls if promoted

| Control | Frequency | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
